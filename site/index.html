easymesh-clients

The Wi-Fi clients of the EasyMesh labs

A mesh is judged by what happens to its clients. Every lab therefore has them: small containers, each with one simulated radio, that join the mesh as a phone or a laptop would. Each of the two optimizer labs keeps a hundred. This page explains what a client is, how it is built, configured and managed, what it does today, and how it can be made to do more without breaking a room that works.

What a client is

In the virtual labs nothing is hardware. The gateway and the extenders are containers, and their radios are simulated by the Linux kernel (mac80211_hwsim). What one radio hears of another is decided by the RF medium, which knows where everything stands in a room and what the walls are made of.

A client is the smallest thing that can join such a mesh: a container with one simulated radio, the standard Linux Wi-Fi client program (wpa_supplicant) and an address. To the mesh it is a station like any phone. It associates, authenticates, gets an address, answers when it is pinged, and moves to another access point when the mesh asks it to.

Clients are in almost every test the labs run. The optimizer exists to move them, the medium is measured through them, and a room passes or fails by where its clients end up. Yet until now they were described nowhere: each lab grew its own.

How one is built

A client starts from an image that the lab builds once. In the RDK lab it is Alpine Linux with two packages and one file added:

  • iw, the kernel's Wi-Fi tool, and the supplicant package for its libraries and wpa_cli;
  • the lab's own build of wpa_supplicant 2.10;
  • a start script that connects whenever the container starts.

The lab builds its own supplicant for one reason. A mesh steers a client by sending it a BSS Transition Management (BTM) request, part of 802.11v: "please move to this access point". The supplicant that Alpine ships is built without the code that handles it, so it receives the request and stays where it is, without answering. A client that cannot be steered is of no use to a steering lab. The lab's build switches that code on (CONFIG_WNM), and carries one small patch so a steer also finds a network whose name is hidden.

Each client then gets a radio from the VM's pool of simulated radios, handed over as its wlan0, and a limit of 128 MB of memory and one CPU. A hundred of them fit next to a gateway and four extenders in one lab VM.

Why containers

A simulated radio belongs to one network namespace. A container is the cheapest thing that has one, with its own address, routes and processes, so each client is a separate device to the mesh and to the tools that look at it.

How one is configured

A client knows one network. Three choices decide how it joins, and they are all it differs in from the client next to it.

ChoiceValuesWhat it means
Networkprivate_ssid, iot_ssidthe home network, or the IoT network, which is not announced and has to be asked for by name
Securityopen, WPA2, SAESAE (WPA3) comes with protected management frames, and is required on 6 GHz
Bandany, 2.4, 5 or 6 GHza band-limited client scans and joins only on that band's frequencies

In the RDK lab one command creates a client, and another creates the whole pool:

gen/wlan-client.sh -i 7 --band 5 up private_ssid <passphrase>   # one client: wlan-client-007
gen/wlan-client-pool.sh plan                                      # what the pool would create
gen/wlan-client-pool.sh up                                        # all 100

The pool is always the same hundred: fifty on the home network and fifty on the IoT network. Nearly all are WPA2 on any band; one is limited to 2.4 GHz and one joins on 6 GHz with SAE. A room does not create clients. It chooses which of the hundred are online.

What a client was created as is written on its container, as four keys, so a tool can ask instead of guessing from a MAC address:

user.easymesh.cohort     private
user.easymesh.ssid       private_ssid
user.easymesh.security   wpa2
user.easymesh.band       5

Joining the mesh

Starting a client is not the same as having one. The labs accept a client only after it has passed four gates, in order, each with a time limit.

The fourth gate exists because of a real failure. Right after a client associates, the controller learns what the client can do in a short exchange. If that exchange is lost, the client is associated and yet missing from the controller's model until it roams. So the lab accepts neither a client nor a pool until the controller lists it.

The address comes from the gateway's DHCP server in the RDK lab, where the client asks once at start. The prplMesh lab gives each client a fixed address instead and runs no DHCP.

What a client does today

Less than a phone, and that is deliberate so far. The lab's client is the easiest client an optimizer can meet.

It doesIt does not
join its one network and keep tryingchoose between networks
answer a pingsend any traffic by itself
move when the mesh asks, every time it can see the targetrefuse, ignore or delay a request
stay where it is while the signal fadeslook for a better access point by itself
behave exactly like the other ninety-nineresemble any particular kind of device

The fourth row surprises people. A real phone scans in the background and roams when its signal drops. The lab's client has no background scanning configured, so it reconsiders its access point only when something else makes it scan: a steering request, a disconnect, or the room asking for a scan. Carry it across the room and it holds on until the mesh moves it.

What that means for results

An optimizer that does well here has been tested against obedient clients that never act on their own. Real networks have clients that roam early, clients that refuse, and devices that know one band. A good result in the lab is necessary, and not yet sufficient.

Clients in a room

A room is a floor plan with a mesh and clients in it, played over time. Its description, a world, never names a container. It names roles, such as sta_static_04 for a client that stays put and sta_mobile_02 for one that walks. Each lab keeps a small file that binds roles to its own containers, which is how one world runs on both labs.

A room manages its clients in three ways.

  • Presence. The whole pool is online to begin with. A client the room does not use is disconnected and noted as paused, so that it is reconnected when the room ends, or after an interruption. The default room keeps twenty clients online; the largest use fifty.
  • Bands. A world may limit up to four of its clients to bands, for band-steering rooms. The room saves the client's own settings first and writes them back afterwards.
  • Traffic. A world may give phases of load. The room runs ping and iperf3 for the client, from the lab VM, inside the client's network.

Reaching into a client

A client has no agent, so everything that wants something from it goes in from the lab VM. Four groups of tools do, each with its own code.

There are two ways through that door, and the difference decides what a client image must contain. lxc exec runs a program that is in the client. Entering only the client's network (nsenter) runs a program that is in the lab VM, with the client's radio and address. The room's traffic already works the second way, which is why the RDK client needs no iperf3 of its own.

Growing without breaking

From here on: proposals

Everything above exists. Everything below is proposed, and none of it is built.

The clients could do more in two directions: do something when asked, and behave like a kind of device. Both are worth having, and both could easily disturb rooms that pass today. So one rule comes first:

A client nobody asks anything of is today's client. No existing room, world, suite or lab script changes. Each step adds something, and a lab takes it when it wants it.

The first step is the dullest and the most useful. The two optimizer labs' clients have drifted apart because nothing said what a client must provide. A short contract (the radio is wlan0, wpa_cli works, the address is on wlan0, what it was created as is written on the container) lets one tool work on both. Everything beyond that runs from the lab VM inside the client's network, so no client image ever needs a tool added.

Asking a client to do something

Today, getting two clients to send traffic to each other means knowing two container names, two addresses, which lab has iperf3 where, and the right flags. The proposal is a small vocabulary instead: one command on the lab VM that takes a client by its container name, or by its role in the room.

em-clients status sta_mobile_02                              # where is it, how well does it hear
em-clients scan   sta_mobile_02                              # what else can it hear
em-clients iperf  sta_mobile_01 sta_static_03 --seconds 10   # traffic between two clients
em-clients roam   sta_mobile_02 <bssid>                      # the client moves by itself

No agent, and no wire

The obvious design is a small service in each client, listening on a wired interface. It is not needed, and it would cost something. The lab VM can already run anything in any client; an agent could do nothing more. A wired interface with an address gives the client a second way out, and a Wi-Fi client with a second way out can send traffic around the Wi-Fi it is there to test. The OpenSync lab's client and the Protocol lab's clients have no wire for exactly that reason.

Someone who is not on the lab VM, a remote developer or a browser, would reach the same actions through the room service, which then also knows what was done.

Not disturbing a room

A room owns its clients while it runs, and its checks assume nobody else touched them. Actions therefore come in three classes.

ClassExamplesWhile a room runs
Readlist, status, scanalways allowed
Trafficping, iperfallowed, unless the room is using that client for its own traffic
Statedisconnect, roam, band, modelrecorded in the room's journal; the run is marked touched by hand, and its checks no longer count as a pass

The room's description, and the configurator that compiles it, are not involved at all. That is what keeps this step small.

Clients that behave like devices

The harder questions for an optimizer need clients that are not all alike. Does it stop steering a client that keeps refusing? Does it fight a phone that roams by itself? Does it leave a 2.4 GHz plug alone? A client model makes a lab client roam the way a kind of device documents that it roams.

baselinetoday's lab clientnever by itselfa small, throughput-weighted margin
iphone, ipadiPhone, iPad−70 dBm8 dB with traffic, 12 dB idle
macMac with Apple silicon−75 dBm12 dB
pixelGoogle Pixel−75 dBm, or −70 dBm on a busy AP10 dB
galaxySamsung Galaxy−75 dBm, or −65 dBm on a busy AP10 dB
windows-intela Windows laptop with Intel Wi-Fi−75 dBm (not published; assumed)10 dB (assumed)

The values come from the devices' own documentation: Apple's deployment guide, the source of the Pixel's Wi-Fi driver, Samsung's published roaming rules, Intel's settings, iwd's manual. Each carries its source and how sure it is; what nobody documents is marked as assumed. Variants add a 2.4 GHz-only IoT device and clients that refuse, ignore or cannot receive a steering request.

A model is data. One build of wpa_supplicant 2.12, with a few options added whose defaults keep today's behaviour, serves every model; the same container becomes an iPhone or a plug when a room loads. Only linux-iwd runs a different program, which is its point. Before any of it reaches a lab, a bench of two simulated access points and one client checks each model: where it starts looking, how much better the new access point is when it moves, and how it answers a steering request. The requirements and design, the sources and the test plan are in this repository.

A model on the bench

A model is a claim: this client starts looking at −70 dBm and moves for 12 dB. Before a model goes near a room, a bench checks the claim. Two access points and one client run on simulated radios in network namespaces of a small VM, with the labs' own medium carrying every frame between them. A driver sets each signal level exactly, walks the levels by script, and records everything on one clock: the levels it set, every event of the client and of both access points, and the client's state every second.

Each test asks one question of a model, and the expected answer comes from the model file, so one set of tests serves every model:

TestWhat it asks
B1Where does it start looking, as its access point fades?
B2Does it stay put above its trigger, even when a scan shows a far better AP?
B3, B4How much better must the other AP be before it moves, idle and passing traffic?
B5 to B7How does it answer a steering request (BSS Transition Management): to a better AP, to a weaker one, with disassociation imminent?
B8Does a busy access point make it look, as the Pixel and Galaxy document?
B9Which band does it choose, and does a 2.4 GHz-only device stay there?
B10Does it ping-pong between two access points whose signals keep crossing?
B11, B12Does iwd do what its manual says, and does today's default client still behave as the labs' current build?

On 3 October every model ran every test that applies to it, three times: 441 runs on five copies of the bench VM, ten hours of runs in under three hours side by side. Every conformance test of every model passed, in all three runs. The tests a model does not specify (how it answers a request without the Abridged bit, a disassociation, which band it picks) are recorded on its card.

Every model started looking exactly 2 dB below its trigger, in every run, and moved 1 to 5 dB over its margin, the step of its 10-second scan. None ping-ponged in 33 ten-minute runs of crossing signals. The Pixel and Galaxy models looked when their access point said it was busy; the Mac did not. Today's default client, on wpa_supplicant 2.12 with the patches, did what the labs' 2.10 build does in every test.

Building the bench taught as much as running it:

  • A client is never idle by its packet counters. An access point sends about ten beacons a second, so a client counting received packets looks busy at all times. "Passing traffic" now counts what the client transmits, which is also how Apple words it.
  • Simulated radios scan slowly. Scanning every channel takes 15 to 30 seconds on the simulated radio, against a few seconds on a phone, long enough for wpa_supplicant to give up on the scan. The bench limits scans to its two channels and says so.
  • wpa_supplicant 2.12 obeys a steering request by its form. With the Abridged bit set, the usual form, it follows. Without it, its current access point stays a choice; on the bench, judging from a scan 8 seconds old, it chose it, and "accepted" the request while naming the access point it was already on.
  • The bench caught a mistake of our own. The patched client's accept answer, meant to follow the access point's choice, at first did the same. It now takes the access point's preferred candidate, and the steering tests of every model that accepts were run again.
  • iwd never answers a steering request. It moves, but sends no response: a controller waiting for one waits in vain.
  • hostap's own tests of steering, background scanning, roaming and scanning give the same results with the patches as without them.

The bench, the client build and the results, with a behaviour card per model, are in this repository. No lab uses the models yet; that is the next step, one lab and one room at a time.

A home for the clients

The medium and the optimizer each moved out of the labs into a repository of their own, which both labs now pin. That worked because both labs already ran the same code. The clients are not there yet: they are separate implementations, tied to each lab's way of handing out radios and of waiting for its own controller. Moving them today would mean making them the same and moving them in one go, and every suite of both labs would have to run again.

So this repository starts with what is new, and takes in what exists only when there is a reason.
